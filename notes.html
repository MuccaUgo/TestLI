Chapter 1 · 7 anchors

Introduction

How data becomes useful information and supports an organization’s activities and decisions.

1.1 DIKW Pyramid #

The DIKW pyramid describes how data can support understanding and decisions:

  • Data: raw facts or measurements, such as individual sales records.
  • Information: data interpreted in context, such as “100 iPhones were sold in this store today”.
  • Knowledge: information combined with experience, such as recognizing that sales regularly increase on Saturdays.
  • Wisdom: applying knowledge and judgement to choose an appropriate action, such as scheduling additional staff.

Higher levels summarize and interpret the detailed data below them.

Remember Facts → context → understanding → appropriate action.

1.2 Information in Organisations #

Organizations use information for communication, process execution, control and decision-making. Information can also be a product, such as a market report.

Information has three important characteristics: it is intangible, shareable without being consumed, and self-regenerating, because using it can produce new information.

Organizations create, collect, process, store, transmit and present information. These activities may be performed manually or supported by IT.

Remember Information supports activities and generates further information.

1.3 Anthony’s Pyramid #

Anthony’s pyramid divides organizational activities into three levels:

  • Operational: carrying out daily activities, such as recording sales and updating stock.
  • Tactical — management and control: planning resources and checking performance, such as comparing weekly sales with targets.
  • Strategic: defining long-term objectives and policies, such as deciding where to open new stores.

Strategic goals guide lower levels, while operational results provide information for control and decisions. Departments also exchange information horizontally.

Remember Perform activities → manage performance → set direction.

1.4 OLTP vs OLAP #

OLTP — Online Transaction Processing supports daily operations. It handles many short, repetitive transactions using detailed, current data, with frequent reads and writes.

OLAP — Online Analytical Processing supports decisions through complex queries over historical data, often involving aggregation and comparisons. Recording an iPhone sale is OLTP; comparing annual sales across stores is OLAP.

OLTP performance often focuses on throughput, or transactions processed per second. OLAP performance focuses on query response time.

Database transactions commonly follow ACID: Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation and Durability.

Remember OLTP runs the business; OLAP helps analyse it.

1.5 Data-Driven Decision Making #

Data-Driven Decision Making — DDDM means using data and analysis to support decisions.

Organizations collect and prepare data, analyse it and present findings in an understandable way. Managers use these findings to improve processes, understand customers and identify opportunities.

A store can combine sales and visitor counts to identify busy periods and plan staffing. Reliable decisions require both good-quality data and appropriate analysis methods.

Remember Collect evidence → analyse it → use the findings to decide.

1.6 Moody’s Laws #

The course emphasizes three rules about the value of information:

  • The value of information increases with use: using the same information for different purposes can create additional value without consuming it.
  • Information is perishable: its usefulness can decrease as it becomes outdated. Data that is no longer useful for daily operations may still support historical analysis.
  • More is not necessarily better: collecting more data does not automatically create more value. Some data may remain unused while still requiring storage and maintenance.

Remember Use creates value; time affects usefulness; quantity alone is insufficient.

1.7 Data Governance #

Data governance defines the responsibilities, rules and processes needed to manage data throughout its life cycle, from acquisition to disposal.

An organization must understand what its data contains, where it is located, who can access it, how it is protected, how long it should be retained and how it can create value.

Metadata makes data understandable and discoverable. Governance also addresses quality, security, privacy, compliance and the resources required to store and use data sustainably.

Remember Know your data, control its use and manage its life cycle.

Chapter 2 · 7 anchors

Data Management

How databases organize shared data, enforce rules and answer queries with SQL.

2.1 Structured vs Unstructured Data #

Structured data follows a regular structure, such as a table containing product identifiers, names and prices.

Unstructured data includes free text, images and other information without a fixed tabular structure. Semi-structured data, such as JSON, has a structure that can vary.

Metadata describes the data, including its structure, meaning and allowed values.

Remember Data contains information; metadata explains how to interpret it.

2.2 File Systems vs Databases #

When departments maintain separate files, the same information may appear several times. This causes redundancy and can create inconsistency when one copy is updated and another is not.

Other problems include limited sharing, poor access control and program–data dependence: changing a file’s structure may require changing the programs using it.

A database provides a shared collection of related data and allows redundancy to be controlled.

Remember Shared data reduces duplication and conflicting copies.

2.3 DBMS Functions & Architectures #

A Database Management System — DBMS is the software that manages a database. It supports queries, data changes, access control, concurrent users and persistent storage. It also stores metadata in a data dictionary.

In a client–server architecture, clients request services from a database server. The PAD architecture separates three responsibilities:

  • Presentation: the interface shown to users.
  • Application: business rules and processing logic.
  • Data: storage, retrieval and management of data.

A university portal displays a form, checks the rules for recording a grade and stores the result.

Remember Interface → application logic → data management.

2.4 Relational Model #

The relational model organizes data into tables, called relations. A column is an attribute, and a row is a tuple. Each attribute has a domain, defining its allowed values.

A schema describes the structure: PRODUCT(product_id, name, price).

An instance is the actual collection of rows stored at a particular moment. The instance changes when products are inserted, updated or deleted.

Remember Schema = structure; instance = current contents.

2.5 Keys & Integrity Constraints #

A key is a minimal set of attributes that uniquely identifies a row. It may contain one attribute or several.

A primary key is the chosen identifier: its values must be unique and cannot be NULL. A foreign key references a key in another table, or in the same table.

For example, Sale.product_id can reference Product.product_id. Referential integrity requires each non-null reference to identify an existing product.

A functional dependency, such as product_id → name, price, means that the identifier determines those values. Other constraints enforce business rules, such as allowing honours only when the grade is 30.

Remember Primary keys identify; foreign keys connect; constraints enforce rules.

2.6 Relational Operations & SQL Queries #

Selection keeps rows satisfying a condition. Projection chooses columns. A join combines related rows from different tables.

In SQL, SELECT chooses columns, while WHERE filters rows:

SELECT p.name, s.quantity
FROM Product p
JOIN Sale s ON p.product_id = s.product_id
WHERE s.quantity > 1;

This query combines products and sales, then returns product names and quantities for sales involving more than one unit. Conditions can be combined with AND and OR; parentheses make their grouping explicit.

Remember Choose columns, connect tables, filter rows.

2.7 CREATE TABLE, CRUD & Constraints #

CREATE TABLE defines a table’s structure, including attributes, types and constraints:

CREATE TABLE Product (
    product_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
    name VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
    price DECIMAL(10, 2) CHECK (price >= 0)
);

CRUD describes operations on stored records: Create → INSERT, Read → SELECT, Update → UPDATE, and Delete → DELETE.

NOT NULL requires a value, while CHECK enforces a condition. WHERE selects the rows affected by an update or deletion.

ALTER TABLE changes the schema; INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE change the data.

Remember Define the structure, then create, read, update and delete records.

Chapter 3 · 7 anchors

Data Quality

How to assess whether data is suitable for use and how to identify and improve its weaknesses.

3.1 Fitness for Use & GIGO #

Data quality means fitness for use: data must satisfy the requirements of the people and processes using it. The same dataset may be suitable for one task and unsuitable for another.

GIGO — Garbage In, Garbage Out means that errors in input data can propagate through processing and produce misleading results. Small errors can grow into larger problems.

Yesterday’s sales data may support a weekly report, while yesterday’s stock levels may be unsuitable for checking current availability.

Remember Quality depends on the purpose.

3.2 Accuracy #

Accuracy describes how closely a recorded value matches the correct real-world value.

Syntactic accuracy checks whether a value belongs to the expected domain. For example, "abc" is invalid in a numeric price field.

Semantic accuracy checks whether a valid value is actually correct. A price of €90 is a valid number, but it is inaccurate if the product really costs €900. One possible measure is correct values divided by total values assessed.

How to improve it: validate types, formats and allowed values; compare values with a reliable reference and correct verified errors.

Grade example: if allowed grades are 1–30 plus 30L, a grade of 35 is outside the domain: syntactic inaccuracy. A grade of 25 instead of the true 28 is a semantic inaccuracy. A domain error may also violate a database constraint.

Remember Validate the format, verify the real value, then correct the error.

3.3 Completeness #

Completeness concerns whether the data needed to represent the real-world situation is present.

A sales record without a required product identifier is incomplete. A simple measure compares the number of available values with the number expected.

We must distinguish missing information from information that does not exist or does not apply. A missing phone number does not necessarily indicate poor quality if the person has no phone.

Remember Is the required information present?

3.4 Consistency #

Consistency means that data respects defined rules and does not contain contradictions.

Rules can concern several attributes or records. A delivery date should not precede the order date. If revenue is defined as price multiplied by quantity, the recorded values must satisfy that relationship. A recorded average that is incompatible with individual grades is another inconsistency.

We can assess consistency by comparing successful rule checks with all checks performed.

Remember Do the values respect the rules and agree with each other?

3.5 Timeliness #

Timeliness means that data is current enough for its intended use.

Two important concepts are currency, meaning how old the data is, and volatility, meaning how frequently the information changes.

Stock availability changes quickly and requires frequent updates. A person’s date of birth is stable, so an older record may still be suitable.

Remember How old is the data, and how quickly does it change?

3.6 Data Profiling #

Data profiling examines a dataset and collects statistics to identify potential quality problems before cleaning.

Typical checks include data types, missing values, distinct values, duplicates, distributions, minimums, maximums and relationships between attributes. Counting records by city may reveal that both “Milan” and “Milano” are used:

SELECT city, COUNT(*)
FROM Sales
GROUP BY city;

Profiling identifies suspicious patterns. An unusual value still needs investigation before being changed.

Remember Inspect and measure before correcting.

3.7 Data Quality Improvement & SQL Cleaning #

Data-driven strategies directly correct existing data. Process-driven strategies improve the processes that create or modify data, addressing the causes of recurring errors.

The main cleaning steps are standardization, error correction and duplicate detection, followed by a new quality assessment. SQL can standardize two names for the same city:

UPDATE Sales
SET city = 'Milan'
WHERE city = 'Milano';

SELECT DISTINCT returns unique combinations of selected values; it does not delete stored records. Detecting records about the same real-world entity may also require similarity checks and human review.

Remember Profile → standardize → correct → detect duplicates → assess again.

Chapter 4 · 7 anchors

Data Analysis Pipelines

How a sequence of preparation and analysis steps turns data from different sources into useful information.

4.1 Data Warehousing Pipeline #

A data warehouse supports business decisions by collecting and preparing data from internal and external sources.

ETL extracts the data, cleans and transforms it, and loads it into the warehouse. Analysis tools then help users compare results across products, locations and periods.

An Apple Store manager can compare monthly iPhone sales across different stores.

Remember Sources → ETL → warehouse → analysis.

4.2 Data Science Pipeline #

A data science pipeline covers the complete life cycle of data. The main stages are acquisition, cleaning and transformation, use or reuse, publication, and preservation or destruction.

A company collects customer data, corrects errors, analyses purchasing behaviour and publishes the results. It then decides which data to preserve or delete.

Data may be reused for further analysis, so its quality, meaning and history remain important.

Remember Acquire → clean → use → publish → preserve or destroy.

4.3 Machine Learning Pipeline #

A Machine Learning pipeline collects and prepares data, trains a model and uses it to produce predictions or other results.

In supervised learning, training examples include known answers, called labels. Classification predicts categories, such as “fraud” or “not fraud”. Regression predicts numerical values, such as future sales.

Overfitting occurs when a model performs well on training data but poorly on new data. Training data must also cover relevant situations to reduce misleading results and bias.

Online models update as new data arrives. Offline models remain fixed between training runs.

Remember Prepare data → train → check performance → use the model.

4.4 Data Sources & Source Selection #

Internal sources come from inside an organization: operational databases, sensors and internal documents. External sources include public datasets, statistical agencies and other organizations.

Choosing a source requires checking its accessibility and licence, data quality, metadata, ease of integration, reputation and cost.

A store could combine its internal sales records with external weather data to investigate changes in customer demand.

Remember Choose data that is relevant, reliable and usable.

4.5 Big Data & the Four Vs #

Big Data creates challenges because of its scale and other characteristics. The four main Vs are:

  • Volume: how much data is produced and stored.
  • Velocity: how quickly data arrives and needs processing.
  • Variety: the different forms of data, such as tables, text, images and sensor readings.
  • Veracity: the reliability and uncertainty of the data.

A retailer may combine large numbers of transactions, continuous sensor readings and customer reviews. Reliable analysis still depends on good data quality.

Remember Amount, speed, forms and trustworthiness.

4.6 Data Integration #

Data integration combines data from different sources into a consistent representation.

We must resolve differences in representation, such as an address stored as text or separate fields; structure, such as different field orders or nesting; and presentation, such as different maximum field lengths.

We must also align semantics, meaning what the data represents. Two columns named price may use different currencies. Sales recorded in euros and pounds require a common interpretation before comparison.

Remember Align both the format and the meaning.

4.7 Data Fusion & Pipeline Tools #

Data fusion combines information referring to the same real-world entity, resolving conflicting or overlapping values.

The main phases are schema alignment, entity reconciliation and fusion. First, make the structures compatible. Then identify records describing the same entity. Finally, combine their information and resolve conflicts.

Two customer records with slightly different names may describe the same person. Pipeline tools support these activities: SQL and Python/Pandas can prepare data, warehouses and lakes store it, and analysis tools present results.

Remember Align schemas → match entities → combine information.

Chapter 5 · 7 anchors

Data Formats & Standardization

How flexible formats and shared standards help systems exchange and interpret data.

5.1 Need for Data Standardization #

Different systems may represent the same information using different names, formats or units. Data standardization creates a common representation so that data can be exchanged, combined and interpreted correctly.

One store records a location as “Milano” and another as “Milan”. Standardization helps recognize that they refer to the same city.

Remember Shared representations make data reusable.

5.2 Variable & Hierarchical Structures #

Data does not always fit naturally into a fixed table. An object may have multiple values, such as several phone numbers; nested information, such as an address containing a city and a postcode; or optional attributes that other objects do not have.

Name–value pairs allow new properties to be added when needed.

Remember Multiple values, nested objects and changing attributes.

5.3 JSON #

JSON — JavaScript Object Notation is a text format used to store and exchange data. It represents information through name–value pairs. Curly brackets {} define objects, while square brackets [] define arrays.

Objects and arrays can be nested:

{
  "name": "Anna",
  "address": {"city": "Milan"},
  "phones": ["111", "222"]
}

Here, address is a nested object and phones contains multiple values.

XML also represents hierarchical information, but uses tags, for example: <person><name>Anna</name></person>.

Remember JSON uses objects, arrays and name–value pairs.

5.4 GeoJSON #

GeoJSON uses JSON to represent geographical information. A Feature combines a geometry, describing its position or shape, with properties, describing its characteristics.

{
  "type": "Feature",
  "geometry": {
    "type": "Point",
    "coordinates": [9.19, 45.46]
  },
  "properties": {
    "name": "Example shop"
  }
}

Coordinates are written as longitude first, latitude second. Geometry types include Point, LineString and Polygon. A FeatureCollection contains multiple features.

Remember Geometry tells us where; properties tell us what.

5.5 Spatial Data Quality #

Geographical data can contain several quality problems. Granularity concerns the level of detail: a city and a region are different geographical levels. Different names may identify the same place, such as “Milan” and “Milano”. Conversely, the same name may refer to different places.

Coordinates may also be missing, inaccurate or insufficiently precise. We improve quality by standardizing names, checking coordinates and identifying the correct geographical entity.

Remember Correct place, consistent detail, reliable coordinates.

5.6 Reference Values & Geographic Standards #

Reference values provide shared codes and identifiers that reduce ambiguity. ISO 3166-1 provides country codes in three forms. For Italy, these are IT, ITA and 380.

These codes help different systems recognize the same country. Other sources, such as Wikidata and OpenStreetMap, provide identifiers for geographical entities, although their identifiers are not equivalent to international standards.

Remember Shared identifiers make matching more reliable.

5.7 NoSQL DBMS #

NoSQL databases support flexible data models, including documents and other structures beyond traditional relational tables. Many support distributed storage and scalability as data volumes increase.

MongoDB and CouchDB are examples of document databases that store JSON or JSON-like documents. They can represent nested objects and records with different attributes.

Flexible structures still require clear meanings, validation and quality controls.

Remember Flexible models for storing and managing varied data.

Chapter 6 · 7 anchors

Smart Objects & Time Series

How physical objects are identified, located and observed over time.

6.1 IoT & Smart Objects #

The Internet of Things (IoT) connects physical objects to information systems. A smart object can collect information through sensors, process it and communicate with other devices or services. Some objects can also act on the environment through actuators.

An object may connect to the Internet directly or through a gateway. The data may describe its identity, position or changing conditions, such as temperature. In a shop, a connected sensor can measure room temperature and send readings to a monitoring system.

Remember Object → sensing → communication → useful action.

6.2 Object Identification #

Identification tells us which object we are dealing with. An identifier, such as a serial number, product code or vehicle plate, must be unique within its intended context. A product code may identify a product type; a serial number can identify one individual item.

Distinguish static information, such as the model of a device, from dynamic information, such as its current temperature or position. Knowing an object’s identifier does not automatically tell us where it is. Localization requires additional information.

Remember Identification = which object? Localization = where is it?

6.3 RFID #

Radio-Frequency Identification (RFID) uses radio signals to exchange information between a tag attached to an object and a reader. Both use antennas. Unlike an optical barcode, a tag does not need to be directly visible to the reader, although materials and the environment can affect communication.

A passive tag gets its power from the reader’s signal. An active tag has its own battery and generally supports longer-range communication at a higher cost. A shop can use RFID to identify tagged products and support inventory management.

Remember Tag + reader + radio; passive = reader-powered, active = battery.

6.4 NFC #

Near Field Communication (NFC) enables communication over a very short distance, typically a few centimetres. Bringing devices close together makes the interaction deliberate, as in a contactless payment.

The course distinguishes three modes: reader/writer, where a device reads or writes a tag; card emulation, where a device behaves like a contactless card; and peer-to-peer, where compatible devices exchange information. Actual support depends on the device and platform. NFC provides the communication channel; applications still need suitable authentication and security controls.

Remember A close tap: read a tag, emulate a card or exchange data.

6.5 Localization & GPS #

Localization estimates where an object is. A reader installed at a known location can tell us that a detected tag is nearby. Other systems estimate position using signals from several known reference points.

GPS uses signal travel times to estimate distances from satellites. This is trilateration, based on distances, rather than triangulation, based on angles. A standard solution uses at least four satellites to estimate three-dimensional position and correct the receiver’s clock error. GPS generally works better outdoors, where satellite signals are less obstructed. [1]

GPS needs precise timing for a position fix. A sequence of timestamped GPS positions then forms a time series.

Remember GPS: travel time → distance → position; repeated positions → time series.

6.6 Beacons & QR Codes #

A Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) beacon broadcasts a signal containing an identifier. A nearby phone or other receiver can use it to infer proximity and trigger an application action. For example, a museum app can show information about the exhibit near a beacon. Signal strength gives an estimate, not a perfectly accurate distance.

A QR code is a two-dimensional optical code that a camera scans. It can contain an identifier, text or a URL. If the code is installed at a known location, scanning it can also provide location context.

Remember Beacon = receive a radio signal. QR code = scan an image.

6.7 Time Series & ISO 8601 #

A time series is a sequence of observations associated with timestamps. Readings can arrive at regular intervals or whenever an event occurs. Examples include hourly shop entries, temperature readings and a vehicle’s GPS positions.

Analysis must consider sampling frequency, missing values, noise, trends and seasonal patterns. Resampling changes the time intervals; smoothing reduces short-term fluctuations. Distinguish the time of the event from the time it was recorded.

ISO 8601 provides a standard date and time representation. In 2026-09-07T10:30:00Z, T separates the date and time, and Z means UTC. An offset such as +02:00 specifies a difference from UTC. Normalize time zones before comparing observations.

Remember A value becomes a time series observation when it has a time.

Chapter 7 · 7 anchors

Data Architectures

How data systems are organized, how organizations share data and how its history is recorded.

7.1 Reference Architectures — NIST #

A reference architecture is a general model of the roles and components needed in a system. It provides a common language for designing solutions, rather than prescribing one specific product.

The NIST Big Data model identifies five roles: Data Provider, Data Consumer, Big Data Application Provider, Big Data Framework Provider and System Orchestrator. Applications collect, prepare, analyse and present data. The framework supplies infrastructure such as storage and processing. The orchestrator coordinates requirements and the overall system. Management, security and privacy apply across the architecture.

Remember Who provides, uses, processes, supports and coordinates the data?

7.2 Data Lakes vs Warehouses #

A data lake stores large amounts of heterogeneous data, including structured, semi-structured and unstructured data. It can retain data close to its original form. Schema-on-read means interpreting its structure when it is used.

A data warehouse contains integrated, prepared data organized for analysis and reporting. Schema-on-write means organizing data according to a defined structure before or during loading. See Chapter 8.

A lake still needs governance. Zones can separate raw data, work in progress, enhanced data and data ready for access. Without quality controls, metadata and ownership, finding and trusting the data becomes difficult.

Remember Lake = diverse data for exploration. Warehouse = prepared data for analysis.

7.3 Metadata, Schemas & Naming #

Metadata is information about data: its meaning, format, units, origin, collection date or owner. A schema describes how data is organized, including fields, types and relationships. Clear metadata helps people interpret data correctly and combine it with other datasets.

Consistent naming also makes data easier to understand. Common styles include lowercase firstname, uppercase FIRSTNAME, snake case first_name, Pascal case FirstName and camel case firstName. The important point is to choose a convention and apply it consistently. Provenance is metadata specifically about the history of data.

Remember Metadata explains data; a schema structures it; names make it readable.

7.4 Federated Data Ecosystems #

A data ecosystem includes organizations, people, technologies and rules involved in producing and using data. In a federated architecture, participants remain autonomous while cooperating through agreed interfaces and standards.

Sharing requires more than a network connection. Participants need common meanings, compatible formats, identity and trust mechanisms, ways to discover available data, and rules for access and use. A federated catalogue can help participants find data held by different organizations. For example, shops, a shopping centre operator and a transport provider could combine information to understand visitor flows.

Remember Independent participants cooperate through shared rules and interfaces.

7.5 Data Spaces, Gaia-X & Sovereignty #

A data space is an environment in which participants share and use data under common governance and interoperability rules. Data sovereignty concerns participants’ ability to control access to their data and the conditions of its use. It does not mean that data can never move.

The course presents Gaia-X as a European initiative supporting a federated, trusted data ecosystem.

Shopping centre example: the centre shares hourly visitor counts, and shops combine them with sales. This can be part of a data space if common rules, permissions and interoperable services support the exchange. Sharing one spreadsheet alone does not establish a data space.

Remember Data space = shared data + common rules + participant control.

7.6 Data Provenance #

Data provenance records where data came from, what happened to it and who or what was responsible. It helps users assess quality, trace errors, reproduce results and understand a dataset’s reliability.

For example, a monthly sales total comes from daily sales files that were cleaned and aggregated by a process managed by an analyst. Provenance links the final result to those inputs and activities.

The course considers provenance at different levels: metadata, information systems, workflows and individual data items. More detailed provenance gives more precise traceability but also requires more information to be recorded.

Remember Where did it come from, how did it change, and who was involved?

7.7 W3C PROV & Datasheets #

W3C PROV describes provenance using three main concepts: an Entity, such as a file; an Activity, such as data cleaning; and an Agent, such as an analyst or organization. Relations describe what an activity used, what was generated by it, what was derived from other data and which agent was involved.

Example: a cleaning activity uses a raw sales file and generates a cleaned file, with an analyst associated with the activity.

Datasheets for datasets explain motivation, composition, collection, preprocessing/cleaning/labelling, uses, distribution and maintenance. PROV structures the history of data; a datasheet gives readers broader context for deciding how to use it.

Remember PROV: entity, activity, agent. Datasheet: context and intended use.

Chapter 8 · 7 anchors

Data Warehouses

From everyday transactions to historical, multidimensional analysis.

8.1 OLTP vs OLAP #

Online Transaction Processing (OLTP) supports daily operations, such as recording the sale of an iPhone. It handles many short transactions involving detailed, current data and frequent inserts or updates.

Online Analytical Processing (OLAP) supports decisions through complex queries over historical data, often using aggregates. A manager comparing revenue across stores and years is performing analysis.

A warehouse is commonly described as subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant and non-volatile. It organizes data by business subjects, reconciles sources, keeps history and supports stable analysis through controlled loading. Non-volatile does not mean that the system can never be refreshed. FASMI summarizes analytical expectations: Fast Analysis of Shared Multidimensional Information.

Remember OLTP records the sale. OLAP explains sales performance.

8.2 Facts, Measures, Dimensions & Hierarchies #

A fact represents a business event or phenomenon to analyse, such as a sale. Measures are its numerical values, such as quantity and revenue. Dimensions describe the perspectives used to analyse it, such as date, product and store.

A hierarchy connects levels of detail: day → month → year, or store → city → region. The grain states exactly what one fact record represents. For example, one record might describe sales of one product in one store on one day.

Define the grain first so that measures and dimensions describe the same level of detail.

Remember Fact = what happened; measure = how much; dimension = by what?

8.3 DW Architecture & Data Marts #

Data comes from internal operational databases and external sources. ETL prepares it for the warehouse, possibly using an intermediate staging area. In the course’s terminology, the architecture has two levels without staging and three levels with staging.

A staging area is a temporary working area for collecting and preparing source data. A data mart is a focused collection of analytical data for a department or topic, such as sales or inventory, often derived from a warehouse.

A sales dashboard that updates every two hours does not reveal whether a staging area exists. That delay could simply reflect the refresh schedule.

Sources → ETL (optional staging) → Warehouse → Data marts / analysis

Remember Staging prepares data; the warehouse integrates it; a mart focuses on one area.

8.4 ETL & Data Preparation #

Extract, Transform, Load (ETL) moves and prepares data for the warehouse. Extract reads data from source systems, either in full or incrementally. Transform cleans, standardizes and reconciles it. Load writes the prepared data into the target structure.

Transformation can correct formats, align product codes, remove duplicates, handle missing values, join sources and aggregate records. It is more than removing unnecessary columns.

Apple Store example: transactions record iPhone, Mac and iPad sales. ETL standardizes the store and product information and loads the warehouse. A dashboard then compares revenue across days, weeks and years. These are illustrative examples, not a description of Apple’s internal systems.

Remember Extract from sources → transform for consistent meaning → load for analysis.

8.5 Dimensional Fact Model — DFM #

The Dimensional Fact Model (DFM) is a conceptual model for multidimensional data. A rectangle represents the fact and contains its measures. Connected circles represent dimensions, and branches connect the levels of their hierarchies.

A branch is a path in the diagram. For a sales fact with quantity and revenue, a date branch could be Day → Month → Year; a location branch could be Store → City → Region. Following a branch moves between levels of detail.

The DFM describes the business meaning of the analysis before choosing a physical implementation such as a relational star schema.

Remember Rectangle = fact and measures; circles and branches = analysis perspectives and levels.

8.6 ROLAP & Star Schema #

ROLAP means Relational OLAP: it implements multidimensional analysis using relational tables and SQL. OLAP is the broader analytical approach; ROLAP is one way to implement it.

A star schema has a central fact table connected to dimension tables. The fact table contains measures and foreign keys. Dimension tables contain descriptive attributes and hierarchy levels.

For example, Sales(date_id, product_id, store_id, quantity, revenue) links to Date, Product and Store tables. The Date table can contain day, month and year. SQL joins and aggregations then answer questions such as “What was monthly iPhone revenue in each store?”

Remember OLAP is the analysis; ROLAP performs it with relational tables.

8.7 OLAP Operations & DW Life Cycle #

Roll-up aggregates data, for example daily sales into monthly sales. Drill-down moves to finer detail, such as months to days, when that detail is available. Pivot changes the orientation of the analytical view.

Slice fixes one dimension to one value: show only the Milan store while comparing products and months. Dice selects a subset across dimensions: Milan and Rome, iPhone and Mac, January and February. Slice gives one cut; dice gives a smaller sub-cube.

A warehouse develops iteratively: collect requirements, model the data, implement and test ETL and analytical structures, then deploy and maintain them. Refresh schedules, changing dimensions, historical accuracy and query performance require continuing attention.

Remember Roll up = summarize; drill down = detail; slice = one cut; dice = a subset.

Chapter 9 · 7 anchors

Security

What must be protected, how access is controlled and what cryptography can guarantee.

9.1 Security Domains, Vulnerabilities & Policies #

A security domain groups resources subject to common security rules and administration. A vulnerability is a weakness that could be exploited, such as an unpatched service, a weak password or a poorly controlled physical entrance.

A security policy states what must be protected, who may do what and which rules apply. Controls implement those rules through technical measures, organizational procedures and physical protection.

Security must cover people, devices, networks, software and data. Installing encryption alone does not make a system secure if passwords are shared or unauthorized people can access the equipment.

Remember Domain = shared rules; vulnerability = weakness; policy = required protection.

9.2 Threats & Attacks #

A threat is a potential cause of harm. It can be intentional, accidental or environmental. An attack is an intentional attempt to compromise security. Attackers can be outsiders or insiders.

Sniffing captures network traffic. Spoofing falsifies an identity or address, or presents false data as genuine. A man-in-the-middle attack places an attacker between communicating parties to intercept and possibly alter messages. Malware performs harmful actions, while denial-of-service attacks try to make a service unavailable.

Connect each attack to the weakness it exploits and the security property it threatens. An accidental deletion is a security incident, even though it is not a malicious attack.

Remember Threat = possible harm. Attack = a deliberate attempt to cause it.

9.3 Security Properties #

Confidentiality means only authorized parties can access information. Integrity protects information against unauthorized or accidental alteration. Availability means authorized users can access a service when needed. These three form the CIA triad.

Authenticity gives confidence that an identity or source is genuine. Non-repudiation provides evidence that can help prevent a party from denying an action, such as signing a document.

A student’s grades need all of these: other students should not read them, unauthorized people should not change them, and the portal should work when needed. Different properties require different controls.

Remember CIA: who can read, who can change, and can I access it?

9.4 Authentication, Authorization & Access Control #

Authentication checks identity: “Who are you?” Authorization decides permissions: “What are you allowed to do?” Access control enforces those permissions when a resource is requested.

Authentication factors include something you know, such as a password; something you have, such as a security token; and something you are, such as a biometric characteristic. Multi-factor authentication combines distinct factor types. Two passwords are still one type.

A university portal authenticates a student, authorizes access to their own grades and rejects attempts to access another student’s private record. Permissions should follow the least-privilege principle: only the access needed for the task.

Remember Authenticate identity → authorize actions → enforce access.

9.5 Symmetric Cryptography #

Symmetric cryptography uses a shared secret key for encryption and decryption. The sender transforms plaintext into ciphertext; the receiver uses the secret key to recover the plaintext.

It is efficient for large amounts of data. The main practical challenge is distributing and protecting the shared key. If an attacker obtains it, the confidentiality of the protected data is at risk. AES is an example of a symmetric cipher.

Classical techniques include substitution, replacing symbols, and transposition, rearranging them. Encryption by itself does not guarantee message integrity or prove who sent a message; those guarantees need appropriate additional mechanisms.

Remember One shared secret key; efficient encryption, difficult key distribution.

9.6 Asymmetric Cryptography #

Asymmetric cryptography uses a mathematically related public key and private key. The public key can be shared; the private key must be protected.

For confidentiality with a suitable public-key encryption scheme, a sender encrypts using the recipient’s public key, and the recipient decrypts using their private key. For a digital signature, the sender’s private key signs and the corresponding public key verifies.

Hybrid systems use public-key techniques to establish or protect a session key, then symmetric cryptography to protect the main data efficiently. A trusted link between a public key and an identity is essential; this is where certificates help.

Remember Encrypt for the recipient; sign as the sender.

9.7 Hashes, Signatures & Certificates #

A cryptographic hash maps input data to a fixed-length digest. It is designed to make reversing the digest or finding suitable collisions computationally difficult. A hash is not encryption. A plain hash alone cannot authenticate a message if an attacker can replace both the message and its hash.

A digital signature uses a private signing key and is checked with the corresponding public key. Signature schemes usually incorporate hashing. They support integrity, authenticity and evidence for non-repudiation; they do not hide the message.

A digital certificate binds a public key to an identity and is signed by an issuer, commonly a certification authority. Verification must consider the trust chain, validity period and revocation status.

Remember Hash = digest; signature = verifiable authorship; certificate = key–identity binding.

Chapter 10A · 5 anchors

Traditional Project Management

Five anchors for defining a project, planning the work and controlling delivery.

10A.1 Project Fundamentals & Golden Triangle #

A project is a temporary effort that produces a unique result using limited resources. It has objectives, a beginning and an end. Routine operations, in contrast, continue to deliver recurring activities.

The golden triangle links time, cost and scope/quality. These constraints affect one another: adding features may require more time, more resources or a change in other expectations. A project manager must make these trade-offs explicit.

Uncertainty can concern requirements, technology, resources or the environment. The less we know at the start, the harder it is to rely on a detailed fixed plan without later adaptation.

Remember Temporary + unique result + time, cost and scope/quality constraints.

10A.2 Traditional PM & Waterfall #

Traditional project management emphasizes defining requirements, planning work in advance and controlling execution against a baseline. Progress, costs and changes are monitored throughout the project.

Waterfall organizes development into successive phases, such as requirements, design, implementation, testing and deployment. The output of one phase provides the basis for the next. It works best when requirements and the solution are sufficiently understood and stable.

Changes can still happen, but their effects on scope, schedule and cost should be assessed and approved through change control. Compare this with the shorter feedback cycles used in Agile PM.

Remember Define and plan early; deliver through phases; control changes.

10A.3 Planning, Scheduling & Costs #

A Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) decomposes the project scope into manageable parts. A Gantt chart shows activities along a timeline, including their duration, dependencies and progress.

The critical path is the longest-duration path of dependent activities and determines the earliest possible project finish. Its activities have zero total slack: they cannot be delayed without delaying the project, unless time is recovered elsewhere or the plan changes.

Cost estimates may use analogous projects, bottom-up sums of work packages, parametric models or expert approaches such as Delphi. Include internal, external and less visible costs rather than considering only purchases.

Remember WBS = what; Gantt = when; critical path = what cannot slip; estimates = how much.

10A.4 Organization, Resources & Stakeholders #

An Organizational Breakdown Structure (OBS) describes the organization and roles involved in the project. A RACI matrix connects tasks to responsibilities. A resource histogram shows resource demand over time and helps reveal overloads.

RACI responsibilities
RoleMeaning
ResponsiblePerforms the work.
AccountableOwns the result and approves it; normally one per task.
ConsultedProvides input through two-way communication.
InformedReceives updates.

Mendelow’s matrix classifies stakeholders by power and interest: manage high-power/high-interest stakeholders closely; keep high-power/low-interest stakeholders satisfied; keep low-power/high-interest stakeholders informed; monitor low-power/low-interest stakeholders.

Remember OBS = structure; RACI = responsibility; histogram = capacity; Mendelow = engagement.

10A.5 Risk Management #

Risk management identifies uncertain events, assesses their likelihood and impact, plans responses and monitors changes. A risk is uncertain; an issue has already happened.

Common responses to negative risks are avoid, mitigate, transfer and accept. Prevention reduces the probability of an event; protection reduces its consequences. For example, testing a backup process helps reduce the impact of data loss.

A risk matrix helps prioritize risks using probability and impact. A risk response plan records actions, owners and resources. Revisit both during the project because new risks emerge and existing risks change.

Remember Identify → assess → respond → monitor; give each important risk an owner.

Chapter 10B · 5 anchors

Agile Project Management

Five anchors for adapting through feedback and organizing work with Scrum.

10B.1 Agile Principles & Values #

Agile develops solutions through short feedback cycles. Iterative work revisits and improves a solution; incremental work adds usable parts. Teams collaborate with customers, deliver frequently and adapt as they learn.

The Agile Manifesto values individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working software over comprehensive documentation; customer collaboration over contract negotiation; and responding to change over following a plan. The items on the right still have value.

Agile requires discipline, planning and clear objectives. Its focus is on learning quickly and delivering useful outcomes, rather than assuming every detail can be fixed at the beginning.

Remember People, working results, collaboration and adaptation.

10B.2 Traditional vs Agile #

Traditional approaches emphasize early definition, detailed planning and controlled progression. Agile approaches emphasize frequent delivery, feedback and continuous adaptation. The choice depends on requirements, uncertainty, constraints and the nature of the work.

In Agile, the objective can remain clear while the detailed solution evolves. Planning happens repeatedly at different levels. Feedback helps the team decide what to do next.

Scrum is a framework often used for Agile work. It organizes work into short, fixed-length Sprints and relies on transparency, inspection and adaptation. Its five values are commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage.

Remember Traditional plans from what is known; Agile adapts as the team learns.

10B.3 Scrum Roles #

The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing product value and for effective Product Backlog management, including ordering the work and communicating the product goal.

The Scrum Master helps people understand and use Scrum, supports team effectiveness and helps remove impediments. This role is not a boss who assigns every task.

The Developers create a usable Increment, plan the Sprint work, maintain quality and adapt their plan as they learn. The Scrum Team is self-managing and cross-functional. Responsibilities associated with a traditional project manager are distributed across Scrum accountabilities rather than simply assigned to the Scrum Master.

Remember Product Owner = value; Scrum Master = effectiveness; Developers = usable result.

10B.4 Scrum Artifacts #

The Product Backlog is an ordered, evolving list of work needed to improve the product; its commitment is the Product Goal. The Sprint Backlog contains the Sprint Goal, selected items and the plan for delivering them. Its commitment is the Sprint Goal.

An Increment is an integrated, usable step toward the product goal. Its commitment is the Definition of Done, the shared quality standard. Work that does not meet it is not part of a Done Increment.

A user story can use “As a [role], I want [capability], so that [benefit].” Item-specific acceptance criteria differ from the shared Definition of Done. The Sprint Backlog can evolve while protecting the Sprint Goal.

Remember Product Backlog = overall work; Sprint Backlog = current plan; Increment = Done result.

10B.5 Scrum Events & Monitoring #

The Sprint contains the other Scrum events and lasts one month or less. Sprint Planning establishes why the Sprint is valuable, what can be done and how. The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan.

The Sprint Review inspects the outcome with stakeholders and considers future adaptations. The Sprint Retrospective identifies ways to improve quality and team effectiveness. Review concerns the product and its context; retrospective concerns how the team works.

A burndown chart shows remaining work over time. Velocity can help a team forecast using completed work, often measured in story points; it is not a fair ranking of different teams.

Remember Plan → coordinate daily → review the outcome → improve the process.

Chapter 11 · 7 anchors

Ethical Issues

How to reason about technology’s effects and protect people throughout the data life cycle.

11.1 Ethical, Social & Political Issues #

Technology can create new possibilities before rules and social habits have time to adapt. The same system can improve life for some people and disadvantage others. Ethical questions therefore concern more than whether a system works.

The course identifies five moral dimensions: information rights and privacy; property and intellectual property; accountability and control; system quality; and quality of life. Social and political decisions also influence who receives benefits, who bears risks and which uses are permitted.

For example, a data platform may make services faster while increasing surveillance or excluding people whose data is incomplete. A good analysis makes those effects visible.

Remember New possibilities can arrive before rules; ask who benefits, who is exposed and who can control the system.

11.2 Privacy, Tracking & Profiling #

Privacy concerns people’s ability to control information about themselves and the contexts in which it is used. A service may collect location, clicks, purchases or device identifiers. Each item can appear harmless, but combining data can reveal habits, relationships and sensitive attributes.

Tracking follows a person or device over time. Profiling combines observations to infer a person’s interests, behaviour or likely characteristics and may influence prices, access or opportunities. The inference can be wrong and the person may not know that it is being made.

Ethical design asks whether collection is necessary, whether the purpose is clear, whether people can exercise meaningful control and whether access, retention and sharing are limited.

Remember Many small observations can become a sensitive profile when combined.

11.3 Ethical Principles & Five-Step Analysis #

Ethical decisions should use explicit principles and a structured analysis rather than personal intuition alone. Useful principles include the Golden Rule (“treat others as you would want to be treated”), Kant’s respect for people as ends, utilitarianism (consider overall benefits and harms), risk aversion and the idea that there is no free lunch when benefits have hidden costs.

  1. Facts: what is happening and what could happen?
  2. Values: could it create disparity, injustice or harm?
  3. Stakeholders: who is affected and who has duties?
  4. Options: what decisions or safeguards are available?
  5. Consequences: what will follow from each option?

Revisit assumptions and include people who may be affected indirectly, not only the organization making the decision.

Remember Facts → values → stakeholders → options → consequences.

11.4 Responsibility, Accountability, Liability & Codes #

Responsibility means accepting duties and taking care in decisions. Accountability means that someone can explain and answer for a decision. Liability concerns legal responsibility and possible redress when harm occurs. These ideas are related but not interchangeable.

An organization cannot simply transfer responsibility to an algorithm. Duties can be distributed among developers, managers, deployers and operators, but people and organizations remain responsible for how a system is designed and used. Clear roles, documentation, review and escalation make accountability real.

Professional codes, such as the ACM Code of Ethics, emphasize avoiding harm, being fair and honest, respecting privacy and intellectual property, and improving the quality of professional work.

Remember An algorithm can make a decision; people and organizations still answer for its use.

11.5 AI Risk & Ethical Development #

The course presents a risk-based model. Unacceptable risk covers uses whose ethical implications are too dangerous, such as social scoring, and which must not be deployed. High-risk systems can affect safety, rights or important opportunities, for example medical or safety components and some decisions about education or employment. They need strong controls, testing, documentation, data quality and human oversight.

Limited-risk systems mainly require transparency, such as telling people when they are interacting with a chatbot or viewing generated content. Minimal-risk uses, such as an ordinary spam filter, do not receive additional obligations in this four-level course model, although other laws and good practice still matter.

Ethics must be part of the whole development life cycle: define the purpose, assess impacts, test, monitor, respond to incidents and retire the system responsibly.

Remember The greater the possible harm, the stronger the duties, controls and oversight.

11.6 GDPR Definitions, Roles & Principles #

Personal data relates to an identified or identifiable person. Special categories, such as health or biometric data used for identification, receive stronger protection. The controller decides the purposes and essential means of processing; a processor handles data on the controller’s behalf. A Data Protection Officer advises, monitors and helps communicate compliance; the role does not replace the controller’s responsibility.

The seven principles are lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimization; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability. Accountability means being able to demonstrate compliance, not merely claiming that an algorithm is responsible.

Processing needs a lawful basis, such as consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task or legitimate interests. Consent is one basis, not the only one. Privacy by design and by default make protection part of the system from the start.

Remember Purpose, minimum data, correct data, limited retention, security and demonstrable responsibility.

11.7 Data-Subject Rights & Compliance #

People have rights including clear information, access to their personal data, rectification of inaccurate data, erasure in applicable circumstances, restriction of processing, data portability and safeguards around certain solely automated decisions. Rights have conditions and exceptions; they are not always absolute.

Organizations should document purposes and lawful bases, provide privacy information, control processors, apply appropriate security and keep records that demonstrate compliance. A serious personal-data breach may need to be notified to the supervisory authority within 72 hours when the GDPR notification duty applies, and international transfers need appropriate safeguards.

Compliance is continuous. It includes assessing risks before processing, designing protections, training people, monitoring systems, responding to requests and incidents, and reviewing whether the original purpose and retention period still apply.

Remember Inform people, let them exercise their rights, and keep protection active throughout the data life cycle.
